Tonight, somewhere on Earth, someone will step outside, look up, and feel it. That quiet, ancient question. The same one our ancestors asked around their fires, a hundred thousand years ago.

> vis: deep_field

> pause: 3

For most of history, it was a question for priests and poets. But in the last hundred years, something changed. We built telescopes that can see the edge of time. We sent machines beyond the planets. We learned that the universe is unimaginably old, unimaginably large, and filled with the raw ingredients of life.

> vis: exoplanet_art

## C01 | Where Is Everybody? | Los Alamos, 1950
> lower: Los Alamos, New Mexico | Summer 1950
> vis: archive_los_alamos
The summer of nineteen fifty. Los Alamos, New Mexico. The laboratory that built the atomic bomb is now a quiet town of scientists, high on a mesa in the desert.

> lower: Enrico Fermi | Physicist · Nobel Prize, 1938
> vis: fermi_portrait
Among them is Enrico Fermi. Nobel laureate. Architect of the first nuclear reactor. A man famous for his ability to estimate almost anything, from the power of an explosion to the number of piano tuners in Chicago, with nothing but a pencil and a few clever assumptions.

> vis: archive_1950s
One day, Fermi walks to lunch with three colleagues: Edward Teller, Herbert York, and Emil Konopinski. The conversation drifts to a strange wave of flying saucer reports in the newspapers, and to a cartoon in The New Yorker that blamed aliens for the city's missing trash cans.

> vis: archive_lunch
They joke. They argue about whether faster than light travel could ever be possible. Then they sit down to eat, and the conversation moves on.

> vis: fermi_portrait
Until, suddenly, Fermi asks a question that silences the table.

> pause: 1
> vis: stars_drift
Where is everybody?

> pause: 2
> vis: galaxy_rotation
Those present would later recall that Fermi then ran through a quick calculation. If intelligent life is even moderately common, and if even one civilization learned to travel between the stars, it should have spread across the galaxy long ago. The Milky Way is more than ten billion years old. That is more than enough time for any expanding civilization to reach every corner of it, many times over.

> vis: earth_from_space
So why is there no evidence? Why has no one come?

> lower: Michael H. Hart | "An Explanation for the Absence of Extraterrestrials on Earth" · 1975
> vis: archive_1950s
Fermi never wrote a paper on the subject. He died just four years later. But the question did not die with him. For decades it waited, half forgotten, until in nineteen seventy five an astrophysicist named Michael Hart turned it into a formal argument. If no one is here, Hart wrote, perhaps it is because no one is out there.

> vis: milkyway_timelapse
Others pushed back, and the debate became known by the name of the man who had asked the question over lunch. The Fermi Paradox.

> vis: deep_field
It is not a paradox in the strict, logical sense. It is something more unsettling: a clash between what we expect, and what we see. And to understand why it matters so much, we first need to understand just how strong that expectation is.

> pause: 3

## C02 | A Universe Built for Company | The scale of the cosmos
> vis: earth_from_space
Start with home. Earth, a small rocky world orbiting an ordinary yellow star.

> callout: 100–400 billion | stars in the Milky Way
> vis: sun_sdo
That star, our Sun, is one of somewhere between one hundred and four hundred billion stars in the Milky Way galaxy. If you counted one star every second, without sleeping, it would take you thousands of years to count them all.

> vis: galaxy_zoom
And the Milky Way is just one galaxy. Peer into any small, dark patch of sky with a powerful telescope, and you find it is not empty. It is packed with galaxies, each one a city of billions of suns.

> callout: 200 billion+ | galaxies in the observable universe
> vis: deep_field
Astronomers estimate that the observable universe contains at least two hundred billion galaxies, and possibly far more. Multiply those numbers together, and the count of stars becomes almost meaningless. More stars than grains of sand on every beach on Earth.

> vis: exoplanet_art
For most of the twentieth century, though, we did not know whether any of those stars had planets. It was possible, some argued, that our solar system was a freak accident. That planets were rare.

> lower: 51 Pegasi b | First planet found around a Sun-like star · 1995
> vis: telescope_observatory
Then, in nineteen ninety five, two Swiss astronomers, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, detected a giant planet orbiting the star fifty one Pegasi. It was the first planet ever found around a Sun like star. The floodgates opened.

> vis: kepler_art
In two thousand nine, NASA launched the Kepler space telescope. For years it stared at a single patch of sky, watching more than one hundred and fifty thousand stars for the tiny dimming that happens when a planet passes in front. The results transformed astronomy.

> callout: 6,000+ | confirmed exoplanets
> vis: exoplanet_art
Today, the count of confirmed planets beyond our solar system has passed six thousand. And statistically, the conclusion is overwhelming: on average, every star in the galaxy has at least one planet. Planets are the rule, not the exception.

> vis: habitable_zone
More striking still, a significant fraction of stars host rocky planets in what astronomers call the habitable zone, the band of orbits where temperatures could allow liquid water on the surface. Estimates vary, but there may be billions of such worlds in our galaxy alone.

> vis: trappist_art
Some are startlingly close. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun, hosts a planet in its habitable zone, just over four light years away. The star TRAPPIST one has seven Earth sized worlds, three of them in the zone where water might pool on the surface.

> vis: nebula_dust
And the ingredients of life are not rare either. Water is found everywhere, from comets to the moons of Jupiter and Saturn. Complex organic molecules float in interstellar clouds. Amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, have been found inside meteorites and in samples returned from asteroids.

> vis: earth_from_space
On Earth, life appeared astonishingly early. Within a few hundred million years of the planet becoming habitable, microbes were already here. To many scientists, that speed suggests life may arise easily whenever conditions allow.

> vis: galaxy_rotation
So the stage is set. Billions of worlds. Abundant ingredients. Billions of years of time. If the universe were a lottery, it seems to have printed an almost infinite number of tickets.

> vis: stars_drift
Surely, someone else has won.

> pause: 3

## C03 | The Equation of Ignorance | Green Bank, 1961
> lower: Green Bank, West Virginia | November 1961
> vis: green_bank
In nineteen sixty one, a young radio astronomer named Frank Drake organized a small meeting at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory in Green Bank, West Virginia. It was the first scientific conference ever held on the search for extraterrestrial intelligence.

> vis: radio_telescope_night
Only about a dozen people attended. Among them were a young Carl Sagan, the chemist Melvin Calvin, who learned during the meeting that he had won the Nobel Prize, and John Lilly, a scientist who studied communication in dolphins. Afterward, the group jokingly called themselves the Order of the Dolphin.

> vis: drake_equation
To give the discussion some structure, Drake wrote a single line on the blackboard. It would become one of the most famous equations in science.

> vis: drake_equation
The Drake Equation does not give an answer. It asks a series of questions. How many new stars form in our galaxy each year? What fraction of them have planets? How many of those planets could support life? On what fraction does life actually begin? Of those, how often does intelligence emerge? How often does intelligence produce technology that can send signals into space? And finally, the most haunting factor of all: how long does such a civilization last?

> vis: drake_equation
Multiply these together, and you get the number of civilizations in our galaxy that we might be able to detect right now.

> vis: exoplanet_art
In nineteen sixty one, almost every factor was a guess. Today, thanks to Kepler and other surveys, we have measured the first few with real confidence. We know how quickly stars form. We know that most of them have planets. We know that rocky worlds in habitable zones are common.

> vis: nebula_dust
But the remaining terms are still pure mystery. We have exactly one example of life, and one example of a technological species. You cannot calculate a probability from a single case.

> vis: galaxy_zoom
Depending on what you assume, the Drake Equation can tell you that the galaxy holds millions of civilizations, or that we are the only one, or that even we should not exist. The attendees at Green Bank guessed there might be somewhere between a thousand and a hundred million.

> vis: stars_drift
For this reason, some critics have called it an equation of ignorance. But that is exactly what makes it valuable. It shows us precisely where our knowledge ends. And it shows us something else: the final factor, the lifetime of a civilization, may matter more than all the others combined.

> vis: earth_night_lights
If technological civilizations last only a few centuries before destroying themselves, the galaxy may be a graveyard of brief, isolated flashes. If they last millions of years, the galaxy should be full. Hidden inside the Drake Equation is a question about our own future.

> pause: 3

## C04 | Listening to the Silence | Sixty years of searching
> lower: Project Ozma | Frank Drake · 1960
> vis: radio_telescope_night
In April of nineteen sixty, a year before the Green Bank meeting, Frank Drake had already begun to listen. He pointed a twenty six meter radio dish toward two nearby Sun like stars, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani, and tuned to the frequency of hydrogen, the most common element in the universe, a natural channel any curious astronomer might choose.

> vis: green_bank
He called it Project Ozma, after the princess of the land of Oz, a place, as he put it, very far away, difficult to reach, and populated by exotic beings. For weeks, he listened. There was a brief moment of excitement, a strong signal that turned out to be a passing aircraft. Otherwise, there was nothing.

> vis: archive_1970s
It was the beginning of a search that has now lasted more than sixty years.

> lower: Big Ear Radio Observatory | Ohio State University · August 15, 1977
> vis: radio_telescope_night
On the night of August fifteenth, nineteen seventy seven, the Big Ear radio telescope at Ohio State University picked up something extraordinary. A narrow band signal, far stronger than the background noise, from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. It lasted about seventy two seconds, exactly as long as a fixed source would take to drift through the telescope's beam as the Earth turned.

> vis: wow_signal
A few days later, volunteer astronomer Jerry Ehman was reviewing the computer printouts. He saw the sequence of characters, six E Q U J five, circled it in red ink, and wrote a single word in the margin.

> vis: wow_signal
Wow.

> pause: 2
> vis: stars_drift
Astronomers returned to that patch of sky again and again, for decades. The signal never repeated. Recent research has suggested a natural explanation: a sudden brightening of a cold hydrogen cloud, struck by energy from a passing source. But to this day, the Wow signal remains the most famous unexplained event in the history of the search.

> lower: Arecibo Observatory | Puerto Rico · November 16, 1974
> vis: arecibo
In nineteen seventy four, humanity spoke back. At the dedication of the upgraded Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico, then the largest in the world, astronomers beamed a short message toward the star cluster Messier thirteen, twenty five thousand light years away. One thousand six hundred and seventy nine binary digits, encoding numbers, the elements of life, the shape of DNA, a human figure, and a picture of the telescope itself.

> vis: voyager
Three years later, the twin Voyager spacecraft were launched, each carrying a golden record. Greetings in fifty five languages. Music from Bach to Chuck Berry. The sounds of rain, of laughter, of a mother's first words to her child. A message in a bottle, cast into the cosmic ocean.

> callout: 25 billion km | Voyager 1's distance from Earth
> vis: voyager
Voyager one is now more than twenty four billion kilometers from Earth, the most distant human made object in existence. It will not come within two light years of another star for about forty thousand years.

> vis: radio_telescope_night
The search went on. In nineteen ninety two, NASA launched an ambitious listening program, only for Congress to cancel its funding a year later. The SETI Institute carried the work forward with private money. In two thousand fifteen, the billionaire Yuri Milner and the physicist Stephen Hawking announced Breakthrough Listen, a hundred million dollar, ten year program, the most comprehensive search ever attempted.

> lower: Breakthrough Listen | 2015 – present
> vis: radio_telescope_array
Breakthrough Listen has surveyed thousands of nearby stars, the center of our galaxy, and the cores of nearly a hundred other galaxies. It has used artificial intelligence to sift through billions of signals. It even turned its ears toward interstellar visitors passing through our solar system.

> vis: stars_drift
The result, so far, is the same as Drake's in nineteen sixty. Silence.

> vis: ocean_analogy
But is that silence meaningful? The astronomer Jill Tarter, one of the pioneers of modern SETI, offered a famous comparison. Our search so far, she said, is like scooping a single glass of water from the ocean. If you found no fish in that glass, you would not conclude that the ocean is empty. In two thousand eighteen, a team led by Jason Wright recalculated. The amount searched had grown, but only to about one large hot tub, compared with all the oceans of Earth.

> vis: galaxy_zoom
So perhaps the silence means nothing yet. And yet, there is a deeper problem. The Fermi Paradox is not really about radio signals. It is about something much larger.

> pause: 3

## C05 | The Galaxy Should Be Full | The mathematics of expansion
> vis: galaxy_rotation
Imagine a civilization just a little ahead of us, sending out spacecraft at one tenth the speed of light. Not science fiction physics, just a modest extension of technology we can already imagine.

> vis: galaxy_spread
When a ship arrives at a new star system, it builds a settlement. After a few centuries, that settlement sends out ships of its own. Even with long pauses at every stop, the wave of expansion would cross the entire Milky Way, about one hundred thousand light years, in somewhere between a few million and a few tens of millions of years.

> callout: < 1 day | to colonize the galaxy, if its history were one year
> vis: galaxy_spread
That sounds like a long time. It is not. The galaxy is roughly thirteen billion years old. Earth has existed for four and a half billion. If you compressed the galaxy's history into a single year, the colonization of the entire Milky Way would take less than a day.

> vis: probe_art
And a civilization does not even need to travel itself. The mathematician John von Neumann imagined machines that could build copies of themselves. A self replicating probe could arrive at a star, mine an asteroid, build two copies of itself, and send them onward. Within a few million years, there could be a probe in every star system of the galaxy, including ours.

> lower: Freeman Dyson | Physicist · 1960
> vis: dyson_sphere_art
Advanced civilizations might also be visible simply by their size. In nineteen sixty, the physicist Freeman Dyson suggested that a growing civilization would eventually want all the energy of its star, and might surround it with vast collecting structures, a swarm of panels now called a Dyson sphere. Such a star would glow strangely in infrared light, the waste heat of a civilization impossible to hide.

> lower: The Kardashev Scale | Nikolai Kardashev · 1964
> vis: galaxy_zoom
In nineteen sixty four, the Soviet astronomer Nikolai Kardashev proposed a scale for civilizations based on energy. A Type One civilization uses all the energy available on its planet. Type Two harnesses the full power of its star. Type Three commands the energy of an entire galaxy. Humanity, by most calculations, has not yet reached Type One.

> vis: deep_field
A Type Three civilization would be visible across millions of light years. Astronomers have looked. Surveys of tens of thousands of galaxies with infrared telescopes have found no clear sign of galaxies being consumed by engineering.

> vis: earth_from_space
That is the true force of Fermi's question. It is not just that no one has called. It is that no one seems to have done anything at all. No probes in our solar system. No beacons. No stars dimmed by megastructures. In a galaxy billions of years old, with billions of habitable worlds, even one expansionist civilization should have left its mark on everything.

> vis: stars_drift
And yet, everywhere we look, the universe appears natural, untouched, empty. Something, somewhere, must be wrong with our assumptions. Over the decades, scientists have proposed dozens of answers. They fall into three broad families. Perhaps life is rare. Perhaps something destroys it. Or perhaps they are out there, and we simply cannot see them.

> pause: 3

## C06 | Rare Earth | Are we a cosmic accident?
> lower: Rare Earth | Peter Ward & Donald Brownlee · 2000
> vis: earth_from_space
In the year two thousand, the paleontologist Peter Ward and the astronomer Donald Brownlee published a book that challenged decades of optimism. It was called Rare Earth. Their argument was simple: simple life might be common, but complex life, animals and intelligence, may be extraordinarily rare.

> vis: galaxy_rotation
Consider everything that had to go right for us. Our Sun sits in a relatively calm region of the galaxy, far from the crowded core where supernova explosions and intense radiation may sterilize planets.

> vis: sun_sdo
Our Sun itself is unusually stable. Many stars, especially the common red dwarfs, erupt with violent flares that could strip away a planet's atmosphere.

> vis: jupiter_juno
Our solar system has Jupiter, a giant that may have shaped the delivery of water and helped stabilize the orbits of the inner planets.

> vis: moon_earth
We have a large Moon, born from a colossal collision, which may help stabilize the tilt of Earth's axis and with it our climate over long spans of time.

> vis: volcano_earth
Earth has plate tectonics, a slow geological engine that recycles carbon and helps regulate temperature over hundreds of millions of years. It has a magnetic field that shields the surface from the solar wind.

> vis: microscopic_life
And then there is life itself. For roughly two billion years, life on Earth consisted of single, simple cells. Then, in an event that seems to have happened only once in the entire history of the planet, one cell engulfed another, and instead of digesting it, formed a partnership. That accident gave rise to the complex cell, the ancestor of every plant, animal, and fungus on Earth.

> vis: ocean_life
Even after that, it took more than a billion additional years for complex animals to appear. And of the billions of species that have ever lived on Earth, exactly one has built radio telescopes.

> vis: dinosaur_asteroid
Our story was shaped by chance at every turn. Sixty six million years ago, an asteroid struck what is now Mexico, ending the age of the dinosaurs and clearing the way for mammals. Had it arrived half an hour earlier or later, striking deep ocean instead, the history of life might have taken a completely different path.

> vis: earth_night_lights
Perhaps intelligence is not an inevitable destination of evolution, but a bizarre and improbable detour. Perhaps the galaxy is full of microbial slime, oceans of simple cells on countless worlds, and nothing that will ever look up and wonder.

> vis: exoplanet_art
Critics point out that the Rare Earth argument rests on a sample of one. We do not know how many of these conditions are truly necessary. Life elsewhere might find entirely different paths. But if Ward and Brownlee are right, then the silence of the universe has a simple, and profoundly lonely, explanation.

> pause: 3

## C07 | The Great Filter | Behind us, or ahead?
> lower: The Great Filter | Robin Hanson · 1996
> vis: stars_drift
In nineteen ninety six, the economist Robin Hanson framed the paradox in a new and chilling way.

> vis: filter_graphic
Imagine all the steps between a lifeless planet and a civilization that spreads across the stars. The right star. The right planet. The origin of life. The complex cell. Sexual reproduction. Multicellular life. Tools and intelligence. Technology. And finally, expansion into the galaxy.

> vis: filter_graphic
Given how many planets exist, and how empty the universe appears, at least one of these steps must be extraordinarily improbable. Somewhere along the path, there is a barrier that almost nothing gets through. Hanson called it the Great Filter.

> vis: earth_from_space
The crucial question is: where is that filter? Is it behind us, or ahead?

> vis: microscopic_life
If the Great Filter is behind us, perhaps at the origin of life, or at the birth of the complex cell, then we are among the rare survivors. We passed a test that nearly every other world fails. The galaxy is empty because almost no one gets as far as we have. And the future could be ours to explore.

> vis: earth_night_lights
But if the Great Filter lies ahead of us, then civilizations like ours are common, and something stops all of them before they reach the stars. Every one. Without exception.

> vis: nuclear_archive
What could such a filter be? Since the middle of the twentieth century, humanity has lived with the ability to destroy itself. Nuclear war. Engineered pandemics. Runaway climate change. Technologies we do not yet understand, from artificial intelligence to weapons not yet imagined.

> vis: stars_drift
This is why the philosopher Nick Bostrom once wrote that he hoped our searches for life on Mars would find nothing. If we discovered that simple life had arisen independently on Mars, it would suggest that life starts easily, and that the early steps are not the filter. The barrier would then be more likely to lie somewhere in our future.

> vis: mars_surface
In Bostrom's view, finding fossils on Mars would be the worst news in the history of humanity. Dead rocks and lifeless sands, by contrast, would lift our spirits.

> vis: earth_from_space
It is a strange thought. Every discovery that makes life in the universe seem easier also makes our own future look darker. And every silence makes us look more special, or more doomed.

> vis: galaxy_zoom
We do not know where the filter lies. Perhaps there are several. Perhaps it is not a single wall, but a long series of hurdles. But the idea forces us to ask something uncomfortable: are we looking at the universe, or at our own fate?

> pause: 3

## C08 | The Silent Ones | Hidden in plain sight
> vis: stars_drift
There is a third possibility. Perhaps they are out there, and the silence is a choice.

> lower: The Zoo Hypothesis | John A. Ball · 1973
> vis: earth_from_space
In nineteen seventy three, the astronomer John Ball proposed what he called the Zoo Hypothesis. Perhaps advanced civilizations know about us, but have chosen not to interfere, the way we might watch animals in a nature reserve without disturbing them. Earth might be a protected wilderness, observed but left alone, until we are ready.

> lower: The Dark Forest | Liu Cixin · 2008
> vis: dark_forest
A far darker version comes from science fiction. In his novel The Dark Forest, the Chinese author Liu Cixin imagined the universe as a forest at night, filled with silent hunters. Every civilization fears every other. Since no one can know another's intentions, and since technology can leap forward unpredictably, the safest strategy is to stay hidden, and to destroy anyone who reveals themselves.

> vis: arecibo
In that universe, broadcasting our existence would be the most dangerous thing a young species could do. Some scientists, including Stephen Hawking, have warned against sending loud messages into space before we understand who might be listening.

> vis: deep_field
Others suggest the silence comes not from fear, but from indifference. A civilization millions of years older than us might be as different from us as we are from ants. We do not try to communicate with ant colonies. We do not ask their permission when we build a highway.

> lower: The Aestivation Hypothesis | Sandberg, Armstrong & Ćirković · 2017
> vis: nebula_dust
Then there are more exotic ideas. The aestivation hypothesis, proposed by Anders Sandberg and colleagues at Oxford, suggests that advanced civilizations may be sleeping. Computation becomes more efficient as the universe cools, so a patient civilization might simply wait, dormant, for trillions of years, preserving its resources for a colder, more efficient future.

> vis: black_hole
The transcension hypothesis suggests that civilizations do not expand outward at all, but inward. As technology advances, they become smaller, denser, more efficient, retreating into compact, perhaps even black hole like environments, where we would never notice them.

> vis: digital_universe
Some philosophers go further still, wondering if our reality is a simulation, and the silence simply part of its design. These ideas are difficult, perhaps impossible, to test.

> vis: radio_telescope_night
And there is a more humble explanation. Perhaps they are signaling, but we are not listening the right way. We search for radio, but advanced beings might use lasers, neutrinos, or methods we have not yet discovered. Even our own planet is growing quieter. As we replace powerful television broadcasts with cables, satellites, and digital signals, Earth is becoming harder to detect. A civilization's loud radio age might last only a century.

> vis: stars_drift
Each of these solutions has a weakness. The Zoo Hypothesis requires every civilization, without exception, to obey the same rule for billions of years. The Dark Forest requires all of them to be equally afraid. A single exception, one curious, careless, or expansionist species, would be enough to fill the galaxy. And yet, we see nothing.

> pause: 3

## C09 | Too Early, Too Far | The question of time
> vis: deep_field
Perhaps the answer is not about space at all, but about time.

> vis: galaxy_rotation
The early universe was a hostile place. The first stars contained almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. The heavier elements needed for rocky planets and for life, carbon, oxygen, iron, had to be forged inside stars and scattered by their deaths. It took billions of years for galaxies to become rich enough in these elements to build worlds like ours.

> vis: supernova
Violent events like gamma ray bursts, the most powerful explosions known, may have regularly sterilized large parts of galaxies in the past. Some researchers suggest that only in the last few billion years has the Milky Way become calm enough for complex life to survive for long periods.

> vis: earth_from_space
If so, we may be among the first. Not alone forever, but early. One of the first lights to switch on in a dark house.

> lower: Grabby Aliens | Hanson, Martin, McCarter & Paulson · 2021
> vis: grabby_graphic
In two thousand twenty one, Robin Hanson and his colleagues developed this idea into what they called the Grabby Aliens model. Their argument is subtle. Some civilizations, they suggest, eventually expand rapidly, at a significant fraction of the speed of light, transforming everything they reach. Once a region of space has been claimed, no new civilization can arise there.

> vis: grabby_graphic
This means that anyone who finds themselves still able to look up at a natural, untouched sky must be early. If we had arisen later, the grabby civilizations would already be here, and we would not exist to wonder about them. By their calculations, we may meet such civilizations hundreds of millions of years from now.

> lower: Dissolving the Fermi Paradox | Sandberg, Drexler & Ord · 2018
> vis: stars_drift
In two thousand eighteen, researchers at Oxford, Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler, and Toby Ord, took another approach. They noticed that most people plug single, best guess numbers into the Drake Equation. But our real uncertainty about some factors spans dozens of orders of magnitude.

> vis: drake_equation
When they properly accounted for that uncertainty, the picture changed dramatically. By their estimate, there was a substantial probability, quite possibly better than even, that we are the only intelligent civilization in the entire observable universe. The paradox, they argued, may not be a paradox at all. The silence may simply be what an uncertain universe should look like.

> vis: galaxy_zoom
Distance matters too. The Milky Way is a hundred thousand light years across. Even if a thousand civilizations existed right now, they would on average be separated by many thousands of light years. A signal we send today might not be answered for tens of thousands of years. Civilizations could rise and fall without ever overlapping in time.

> vis: earth_night_lights
Like two fireflies blinking in a vast field, flashing at different moments, never seeing each other's light.

> pause: 3

## C10 | Whispers in the Data | Clues from the frontier
> lower: ʻOumuamua | 1I/2017 U1 · First known interstellar object
> vis: oumuamua_art
On October nineteenth, two thousand seventeen, a telescope in Hawaii spotted something racing through the solar system. It was moving too fast to be bound to the Sun. It had come from interstellar space. It was named Oumuamua, a Hawaiian word meaning a messenger from afar arriving first.

> vis: oumuamua_art
Oumuamua was strange. It appeared to be highly elongated, perhaps like a cigar or a flat pancake. It showed no visible tail of gas or dust like a comet. And yet, as it left, it accelerated slightly, as if something were gently pushing it.

> vis: oumuamua_art
Harvard astronomer Avi Loeb suggested that Oumuamua might be an artificial object, perhaps a light sail pushed by sunlight. Most astronomers disagreed, proposing natural explanations, such as gases escaping invisibly from an icy body. The object was gone before anyone could look closer.

> lower: 3I/ATLAS | Discovered July 1, 2025
> vis: comet_atlas
In two thousand nineteen, a second interstellar visitor arrived, the comet Borisov, a clearly natural object. And in July of two thousand twenty five, a third: an object named three I ATLAS, a large comet, possibly older than our own solar system, swept in from the galaxy's ancient thick disk. Telescopes and spacecraft across the solar system turned to study it. Radio searches, as always, found no artificial signal.

> vis: kepler_art
In two thousand fifteen, citizen scientists studying Kepler data found a star, KIC eight four six two eight five two, later nicknamed Tabby's Star, that dimmed in bizarre, irregular ways, sometimes by more than twenty percent. For a time, some speculated about alien megastructures. Further observations showed that the dimming changed with the color of light, the signature of dust, not solid structures.

> vis: dyson_sphere_art
In two thousand twenty four, a project called Hephaistos searched millions of stars for the infrared glow of partial Dyson spheres. It found seven candidates, all small red dwarf stars with unexplained heat. Follow up work suggested that at least some of them could be explained by distant, dusty background galaxies hiding behind the stars. But the search itself marked a shift. Astronomers now look not only for messages, but for the footprints of engineering.

> lower: K2-18 b | ~124 light-years away · constellation Leo
> vis: k2_18b
Meanwhile, the hunt for simple life has moved into a new era. In two thousand twenty three and again in two thousand twenty five, a team from the University of Cambridge used the James Webb Space Telescope to study the atmosphere of K2 eighteen b, a planet about one hundred and twenty four light years away. They reported hints of dimethyl sulfide, a molecule that on Earth is produced mainly by living organisms, such as ocean plankton.

> vis: jwst
The announcement made headlines around the world. But other teams soon reanalyzed the data, and found the evidence was far from conclusive. ## C11 | The Next Search | The decades ahead
